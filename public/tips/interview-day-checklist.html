A calm interview-day checklist

Nerves come from uncertainty. Remove the small uncertainties the day before and you free up your attention for the actual conversation.

The night before

  • Re-read the job description once and jot the three things it seems to care about most. Those are your themes.
  • Skim your own resume as if you're the interviewer. Every line is fair game — be ready to talk about the oldest bullet, not just the newest.
  • Have two or three specific stories loaded and ready (see the STAR guide). You don't need one per question; good stories flex.
  • Write down two genuine questions to ask them. "What does success look like in the first 90 days?" beats "what's the culture like?"
  • Sleep. A rested brain retrieves specifics; a tired one reaches for "usually."

For a remote interview

  • Test the actual meeting link, camera, and mic an hour ahead — not two minutes before.
  • Put a light in front of you, not behind you, and raise the camera to eye level.
  • Close everything that pings. Silence your phone and put it out of reach.
  • Keep one page of notes visible near the camera — themes and story titles, not scripts.

For an in-person interview

  • Know the route and add buffer time. Aim to arrive in the area 20 minutes early and walk in 5–10 minutes early.
  • Bring a couple of printed resume copies; it signals preparation and saves an awkward moment.
  • Have water. A dry mouth mid-answer is pure distraction.

In the last five minutes

Don't cram. Do one slow round of breathing and re-read your three themes. Remind yourself that an interview is a two-way conversation — you're also deciding whether you want them. That reframe alone drops the pressure a notch.

During the conversation

  • It's fine to take a two-second beat before answering. Thoughtful beats fast.
  • If you don't understand a question, ask them to clarify. That reads as senior, not unsure.
  • When you finish an answer, stop. Trailing on past the point weakens a strong reply.

The best nerve-reducer of all is having done it before. A practice interview the day before turns the real thing into a repeat rather than a first attempt.
